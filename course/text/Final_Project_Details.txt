The case
An association of Napa Valley wineries has hired you to answer one question: do buyers pay more for a
bottle labelled “Napa Valley”, and if so, how many dollars more?
In the US, certified wine regions called AVAs (American Viticultural Areas) may print their name on the
label. The association also wants to know whether its main competitor, Sonoma, earns a price premium,
and whether that premium is larger or smaller than Napa’s.
You will answer with a hedonic price model: the price of a wine is explained by its characteristics (quality
score, age, production volume, region, grape variety). In such a model, each coefficient is the implicit
price of one characteristic, holding the others constant.
The final project follows the following steps, from a single mean to a full regression, before you answer
the final association’s question.

Part B · The Modeling (45 points)
B1 · Continuous Y, no X (5 points). Compute the mean of Price and its 95% confidence interval. Test
H₀: μ = 25 against H₁: μ ≠ 25. Then explain in one sentence why this mean is the estimate of β₀ in the
model Price = β₀ + ε.
B2 · Continuous Y, one dummy X (8 points).
    1. Compare the mean Price of Napa wines with all other wines using the Excel t-test for two
       samples with unequal variances (Welch). State the hypotheses and the decision.
    2. Regress Price on the Napa valley dummy alone. Show that the slope equals the difference
       between the two group means.
    3. The t statistic of the slope is not the same as the Welch t. Which two-sample t-test does the
       regression reproduce exactly, and why?
    4. Given your histogram in A5, which rank-based test from class would be a safer check here? You
       do not need to run it.
B3 · Continuous Y, one X with 3+ groups (8 points).
    1. Create a Region column with six groups (the five AVAs plus Generic California). Run a one-way
       ANOVA of ln(Price) across the six regions and state the decision.
    2. Regress ln(Price) on the five region dummies. Show that SSR, SSE and F match the ANOVA
       table from step 1.
    3. Why must one region dummy be left out of the regression? Interpret the Napa valley coefficient.
    4. If Sonoma were the left-out region instead of Generic California, what would the Napa valley
       coefficient measure, and what value would it take? Answer from your step 2 output, without re-
       running.
B4 · Continuous Y, one continuous X (6 points).
    1. Compute the correlation between Price and Score, and between ln(Price) and Score. Which is
       stronger, and why might that be?
    2. Regress ln(Price) on Score. Interpret the slope in percent per Score point.
B5 · Continuous Y, several X (12 points).
    1. Linear model: regress Price on all 13 explanatory variables. Comment on which variables are
       significant, on R², and on the overall F-test.
    2. Log-linear model: regress ln(Price) on the same 13 variables. Report R² and the F-test. Interpret

    3. For each model, plot residuals (y axis) against predicted values. Compare the two plots: what did
       the log transformation fix?
    4. The Napa valley coefficient in the log model differs from the one in B3. Explain why, using the
       idea of controlling for other variables. Which variable do you think matters most for this change?
    5. Name one variable not in the dataset that could bias the Napa coefficient if left out, and say in
       which direction.

Part C · Conclusions for the Napa association (25 points)

Choose the model from Part B you trust most, and use it to answer the client. Remember that

C1 · Choose your model (4 points). Choose either the linear model (B5.1) or the log-linear
model (B5.2) as the basis for your answers. Justify the choice with at least two pieces of
evidence from Parts A and B, for example the distribution of Price, the fit of the two models, the
residual plots or the significance of the region dummies. State the reference region and the

C2 · Napa premium (5 points). Using your chosen model, state the price premium of the Napa
Valley name compared with Generic California, other things equal, as a dollar amount per bottle,
and say whether it is statistically significant. If your model gives the premium in another unit
first, show how you converted it to dollars, which price level you used, and why that price level
is a sensible choice.

C3 · Napa versus Sonoma (5 points). Compute the Sonoma premium in the same way. Then re-
estimate your chosen model with Sonoma as the left-out region (drop the sonoma dummy, add a
Generic California dummy). Is Napa’s premium significantly larger than Sonoma’s? Report the
relevant coefficient, its p-value and your decision.

C4 · Significant is not important (4 points). Using your chosen model, compute the price
change from producing 10 more units of Cases (10,000 cases), from one more Score point, and
from one more year of Age, in the unit your model gives. Is the Cases effect large enough to
matter for a winery’s pricing? Compare its size with its p-value, and contrast it with a coefficient
that is not significant in your model.

C5 · Premium or quality? (4 points). Does a price premium for Napa prove that Napa wines
are of higher quality? Look at which variables your model holds constant, and state precisely
what the Napa coefficient measures.

C6 · Message to the client (3 points). Write one paragraph of at most 150 words for the
association’s board, with no statistical jargon: is there a Napa premium, how many dollars per
bottle, how does it compare with Sonoma, and what are the limits of this analysis?
