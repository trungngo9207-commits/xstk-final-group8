Why we estimate?

Statistical inference is the ways of drawing
conclusions about population parameters
from an analysis of the sample data
• Sample is only a part of the Population, it
   cannot be expected to give us the exact
   value of the parameter.
• The observed value of a statistic depends
   on the particular sample selected.
• There will be some variability in the value
   of a statistic over different occasions of
   sampling.




               We wish to make generalizations about a population when only a sample will be available

Three Properties of an Estimator
The two terms of the MSE identity, plus what happens as n grows




  NAMING THE TERMS
  Unbiased · Efficient · Consistent
  The first two are the two terms of the identity on the
  preceding slide. The third is what happens to both as
  the sample grows.
  • Unbiased — Bias(θ̂) = 0: the shots surround the centre
  • Efficient — smallest Var among unbiased estimators
  • Consistent — θ̂ → θ in probability as n → ∞
  • MLE is generally consistent and efficient, but not always unbiased
THE LOGIC OF ESTIMATION


Bias and Variance of an estimator

  Bias (độ lệch) is how far the centre of the estimator's
  distribution sits from the parameter.
  Variance (phương sai) is how widely the estimator scatters
  about its own centre.
  The law of Complexity: model cannot be simultaneously
  rigid (to keep variance low) and highly flexible (to keep bias
  low), they must trade off
THE LOGIC OF ESTIMATION


Bias, Variance, and Mean Squared Error

 Bias is the difference between this estimator's expected value and the true value of the parameter being estimated
 Variance is the degree of dispersion (volatility) of the estimators around their own mean value
 They both show how “accurate” the estimation is. But they are different terms.

 Mean Squared Error (MSE) is the average squared difference between the estimated values and the true value

  x̄ inside the band ⟺ the interval around x̄
  covers μ
  Take an x̄ inside the central band and draw an interval

  Take an x̄ outside the band and draw the same interval:
  it does not reach μ.

The Intervals That Fail



5% of samples produce an interval that does not contain μ, and their authors did nothing wrong.

     Those are the samples whose x̄ landed in the two tails. The researcher holding such an interval drew correctly,
 computed correctly and read correctly — and has no way of knowing, because μ is unknown and no diagnostic reports
the failure. The 5% is not an imperfection awaiting correction: it is the quantity the method discloses, and it was chosen
                                      by the analyst at the moment α was chosen.

Z, T AND Χ²


The Unavailability of σ


   The first pivot needs σ. Nobody has σ. Replacing it by s breaks the pivot's distribution, not its
                                            structure.
       A researcher in possession of σ would in almost all circumstances also possess μ, and no estimation would be
      needed. Substituting s puts a second random quantity into the denominator: the pivot becomes a ratio of two
    random things rather than one random thing over a constant. Its distribution is therefore no longer N(0,1), and the
                                              question is what it has become.




                   The substitution is unavoidable, and it is not free. The price is measurable.

  THE PRICE, MEASURED
  A t-statistic (or t-score) measures how many standard errors a

  Lower in the middle, heavier in the tails, symmetric about
  zero. Heavier tails mean a larger quantile, a larger
