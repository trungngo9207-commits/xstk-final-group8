The Pearson Correlation Coefficient
Covariance computed on standardized variables




  NOT A NEW CONSTRUCTION
  r = cov(X,Y) / (s_x · s_y) = cov(z_X , z_Y)
  A statistical measure that expresses the strength and
  direction of a linear relationship between two
  variables
  • Bounded: −1 ≤ r ≤ 1
  • Unit-free: changing units or origin leaves r unchanged
  • r(X,Y) = r(Y,X)

The Spearman Correlation Coefficient
The Pearson Correlation applied to ranking




  BUILT FROM THE FORMULA OF PEARSON
  A statistical test that measures the strength and
  direction of a monotonic relationship between two
  variables
  • Monotonic relationship means that as one variable increases, the
    other variable also tends to increase
  • Spearman's correlation uses the rank order of the data instead of
    the actual raw values, sorts each variable from lowest to highest
    and assigns numbers (1, 2, 3, etc.) based on their positions.
  • It then runs a standard Pearson correlation calculation on those
    ranked values.

Spurious Correlation (Tương quan giả)



  CIRCUMSTANCE 1
  Pure Coincidence. Among 21 coefficients a high value arises by chance alone; among 200 it is a certainty.



  CIRCUMSTANCE 2
  A shared time trend. Any two series that both grow over time correlate strongly, related or not — Session 14 calls
  this a spurious regression.



  CIRCUMSTANCE 3
  A confounded relationship: a hidden third factor influence both variables. This has a real structure behind it. A
  spurious one has none — and its only remedy is not to have gone looking.

INTERPRETING CAUSALITY

  THE SAME FOUR NUMBERS
  Three causal worlds, one set of coefficients
  Three structures are consistent with the same observed
  association between X and Y, and they produce identical
  r, identical slope, identical R² and identical residual
  plots.
  • Direct effect — X → Y
  • Confounding — X ← Z → Y, with no arrow between X and Y
  • Reverse causation — X ← Y




                                 No computation performed on a cross-section distinguishes them.

SIMPSON'S PARADOX


Why the Reversal Is Possible
  THE PARADOX
  Within each industry the direction falls;
  combined it rises
  A statistical phenomenon where a trend or relationship
  appearing within separate groups of data disappears or
  reverses completely when those groups are combined
  • Confounding Variables: A hidden third factor (or lurking variable)
    influences both the grouping and the outcome.
  • Unequal Group Sizes: Aggregated data gets skewed when larger
    groups or groups with extreme baseline rates dominate the
    combined total.
  • Causal Misinterpretation: Looking only at the surface-level grand
    total can lead to false conclusions about cause and effect
